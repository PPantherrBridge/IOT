Illusion of Tomorrow

A Story of Time, Love, and the Paradoxes That Bind Us

By Enoch K

CHAPTER I Hope in Desolation

The dust fell like snow.

It was the first thing you noticed about 2050—not the collapsed buildings, not the military walls ringing the wealthy districts, not the absence of birds. It was the dust. Fine and grey and constant, it settled on shoulders, coated windowsills, gathered in the folds of every garment. Clothing was manufactured now to repel it. Generators used it for fuel. The poor had long since stopped fighting it. They simply breathed it in and went about their shortened lives.

Mikael Carver sat in the third row of Professor Haines’s Environmental Studies seminar and watched the dust drift against the window rather than listen to a lecture he had already read twice. He was twenty-five years old, black-haired, average in every physical way except for the strange flexibility of his elbows and knees—joints that bent a fraction further than they should, a trait that had puzzled every doctor who noticed it. He rubbed his temple with two fingers, an old habit from years of carrying knowledge he could not organize by date.

The dreams again. He hadn’t slept well. He never did.

Since the age of seven, Mikael had dreamed of a man in purple armor. Not the same dream each time—they came in fragments, non-linear, never in sequence. A throne room above the stars. A destroyed planet dissolving into light. A woman with dark greenish-blue skin and alligator eyes watching him from across a corridor. The armored figure, standing at the edge of something vast, turning to look directly at Mikael—not at the camera of the dream, but at him—and smiling with a sadness so old it had become something else entirely.

He had named the figure Kaelim. He didn’t know why. The name had simply appeared in one of the early dreams, spoken by someone whose face he never saw, and it had stuck. His bedroom wall at home was covered in a massive green pinboard with red string connecting eighteen years of sketches, observations, and questions he couldn’t answer. Leather notebooks labeled by age sat in a careful stack on his desk. Age 7–9. Age 10–12. Age 13–15. Each one worn at the corners. Each one full.

He was used to the dreams now. Used to the weight of them. What he was not used to was the girl at the front of the room.

She had been arguing with Professor Haines for four minutes, which was three minutes and fifty seconds longer than anyone else in the seminar had ever dared. Her dark hair fell across her shoulder as she leaned forward over her desk, one hand flat on the surface as though bracing herself against the professor’s resistance.

“The 1973 incident isn’t even in the curriculum,” she said. “Which tells us something, doesn’t it? Because if it were simply an accelerant to pre-existing collapse, there’d be nothing to hide.”

“Miss Engel,” Professor Haines replied, with the patience of a man who had been saying Miss Engel in that exact tone all semester, “environmental collapse is a multi-factor—”

“Multi-factor model, yes. I’ve read the UEC report. All four hundred and twelve pages of it. What I haven’t read is any explanation for why environmental metrics began accelerating at the precise rate they did immediately following a military deep-earth project that was classified within a week of completion. That’s not multi-factor. That’s a cover.”

Mikael set down his pen. He had been about to write something in his notebook—a fragment from last night’s dream, a detail he didn’t want to lose—but he forgot what it was.

After the seminar, she was gathering her notes when he passed her row, and without looking up she said: “You were going to write something and then you stopped. What was it?”

He stopped. “I don’t know. I lost it.”

“That happens to me when something more interesting interrupts.” She looked up. Brown eyes, watchful. “Was I more interesting?”

Mikael considered this with the honest deliberation that people sometimes mistook for slowness. “You were more interesting than what I was writing. Which was about a dream. So the bar was ambiguous.”

She tilted her head. “Can we fix this planet, or are we just studying a corpse?”

“That depends on whether anyone’s willing to say what actually killed it.”

She smiled. It was quick and slightly crooked. “I’m Georgia.”

“Mikael.”

He went home that evening and told Frieda about her. His mother sat across from him at their small kitchen table—blonde hair pulled back, slightly skinny in the way that came from decades of giving food money to other things—and she watched him with the careful attention of someone who had learned to read her son across years of slight distance.

“She knows something’s being hidden,” he said. “About 1973.”

“A lot of people suspect something’s being hidden,” Frieda said.

“She has documents.”

Frieda was quiet for a moment. “Be careful with people who have documents, Mikael.”

That night at his desk, he opened his newest notebook to a clean page and sketched, from memory, the armored figure from last night’s dream—Kaelim, standing at the edge of a burning world, the purple light of him the only warm thing in a cold universe. Around the sketch he wrote the usual questions: Who is Kaelim? Why does he look at me? Why do I feel like I already know the answer?

He closed the notebook. He looked at the pinboard on the wall.

The dreams never stop. They never will.

CHAPTER II The Beginning

Three weeks passed in coffee shops and study rooms, and somewhere inside those weeks they became something that didn’t have a name yet but felt like one was coming.

Georgia always arrived first to their Tuesday-Thursday corner booth at the café three blocks from campus. She always ordered for both of them—not because he needed her to, but because she had very clear opinions about coffee and had decided early on that his were underdeveloped. He had not objected. He had a dry sarcasm about most things but chose his battles, and coffee was not one he cared about winning.

What he cared about was the 1973 incident. So did she. That made the conversation easy in a way conversation had never been easy for him before.

“My mother found a document,” Georgia said one afternoon, her voice dropping to the register she used for things that mattered. “In a museum archive. She’s an archaeologist—she was doing a cataloguing project and found it misfiled. Handwritten, seven pages. A man named Harrens. A geophysicist. He wrote it the night before whatever happened in that facility.”

Mikael’s hands went still around his cup. “What does it say?”

“That he tried to stop them. That what they found in the earth was alive. That he was afraid of what touching it would cost.” She looked at the table. “He died in there. The official record lists him as missing. No inquiry, no cause of death.”

“Do your parents know you have it?”

“My mother doesn’t know the significance of it. She found it and brought it home and forgot about it. I found it when I was sixteen. I’ve been researching since then.”

Mikael looked at her. “Does that mean you’ve known since you were sixteen that you were going to do something about it?”

“Yes.” No hesitation. Then: “Doesn’t that strike you as slightly obsessive?”

“I’ve had a pinboard on my wall since I was seven,” he said. “I’m not in a position to judge obsessive.”

She laughed—a short, genuine thing. He realized he hadn’t heard her laugh before, only argue and deadpan, and the difference was significant.

They chose the 1973 incident as their partner project. Professor Haines approved it with the wariness of a man who knew he couldn’t say no. They worked in the university study rooms with Georgia’s copies of her mother’s classified documents spread between them, building a picture of something the government had spent fifty years making invisible. A geophysicist named Dr. Daryl Harrens. A black-budget facility. Seismic events across three continents. A cover-up so thorough it had become invisible through sheer duration.

“The government broke something in 1973,” Georgia said one evening. “And covered it up. And Earth has been dying ever since.”

One evening, near the end of a long session, Georgia pushed the papers to one side. “My parents don’t know about you.”

He looked up. “Should they?”

“Probably. Eventually.” A pause. “They adopted me. Which I know, and they don’t know I know. I found the documents at sixteen—same year as Harrens’s letter. Productive year.”

“And they’ll have opinions about me specifically.”

“Very specific ones.”

He was quiet. Then: “I kind of need you in my life.”

She looked at him directly. “I need you in mine too.”

He went home that night and did not open his notebooks. He sat at his desk and thought about the pinboard and the eighteen years of questions on it, and for the first time in as long as he could remember, the dreams felt less like the most important thing in his life.

Maybe hope isn’t crazy after all.

CHAPTER III The Announcement

The broadcast came on every screen simultaneously. Director General Harlan Voss of the United Earth Council stood before a bank of cameras with the silver hair and composed authority of a man who had spent decades cultivating exactly this appearance for exactly this moment.

“Mars is habitable. Humanity has a future.”

The Great Martian Migration Mission—the GMMM—was ready to commence. The requirements scrolled beneath his face in clean bureaucratic text: university degree in an essential field, or professional certification with active employment, or financial passage at eighty to one hundred and fifty million rupees per person.

Mikael and Georgia were in the café when it played. Around them, reactions split along familiar lines—those whose futures had just been secured began making plans, and the rest went quiet in the particular way of people absorbing something they already knew was coming but had hoped wouldn’t arrive this soon.

Mikael did the arithmetic immediately. No degree—still a student. No money. His mother Frieda was a nurse who had spent every spare rupee on his education. They were poor and the GMMM required them not to be.

“I’ll talk to my parents,” Georgia said. Her voice was steady but her jaw was set. “They have money. They’re connected. If I tell them—”

“For me and Frieda that’s over two hundred million combined,” Mikael said quietly.

“I won’t give up.”

He wanted to tell her that he already knew how this would end. But she had that expression, the one she wore when she had decided something, and he had learned it was immovable.

That night he sat with Frieda at their kitchen table and told her about Georgia’s plan. His mother listened the way she always listened—quietly, her warm face giving nothing away until she was ready to give something.

“Rich parents with a daughter who loves someone below their station,” Frieda said at last. “That dinner won’t go well, Mikael.”

“I know.”

“You are not lesser. Just different circumstances.”

He went back to his room and sat in the dark for a long time, the notebook closed on the desk beside him.

One dinner. One chance. Please let it be enough.

Outside, across the crumbling city, the GMMM announcement played on loop from every functioning screen.

CHAPTER IV The Dinner

He took three buses to reach the Engel estate. The house was pre-collapse architecture—the kind built when there was still water to spare for private gardens. The hedges were green. The path was clean. A private filtration system, he understood. Wealth could purchase the illusion that nothing had ended.

Georgia answered the door in a simple dress, her dark hair loose. She squeezed his hand once before leading him inside. Her grip said: I’m here. I’m sorry for what’s about to happen.

Dr. Fleming Engel stood when they entered the dining room. He was a well-dressed man, slightly Asian in his features—American-born but with the bearing of someone who had inherited a precise idea of how the world was ordered. His glasses were thin-framed. His assessment of Mikael was complete before Mikael had crossed the threshold. Dr. Helena Matthews, Georgia’s adoptive mother, sat with perfect posture and confirmed nothing with her expression—the expression of a woman who had long since decided that her husband’s conclusions were her own.

The dinner began with conversation about the GMMM. Fleming spoke about the mission’s importance, the UEC’s extraordinary achievement, the necessity of selecting the right people for humanity’s continuation. He did not yet look at Mikael directly. He was establishing the frame.

Then he set down his fork.

“Tell me about your family. Your lineage. Your background.”

Mikael felt Georgia go still beside him. He had prepared for this. “My mother is a nurse. She raised me alone.”

“A nurse.” Fleming repeated it the way a doctor names a diagnosis. Neutral. Clinical. Final. “And your education—still a student? No degree?”

“I’m completing my Environmental Studies program—”

“Which will be,” Fleming said, “worthless. On a dead planet.”

“Father—”

He raised one hand. Not forceful. Simply certain. Georgia went silent.

“My daughter,” he continued, each word placed with surgical precision, “is extraordinary. She has a place on Mars through merit. She will be part of humanity’s future. You are a boy from poverty with no degree, no connections, and no prospects. You have made my daughter love you, knowing you cannot follow where she must go.”

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the dust-filtering system hummed.

“You think you love her,” Fleming said. “You cannot afford passage to Mars. You certainly cannot afford to love a woman so far above your station that you could never, in your wildest dreams, hope to deserve her.”

Mikael said nothing. Not because he had no answer. Because the answer that existed in him in that moment was not something to be spoken at this table, to this man, while Georgia sat beside him with her hands pressed flat against her thighs.

He stood. He looked at Georgia—the tears on her face, the rage in her eyes, the helplessness that was doing battle with both. He held that image for a second that would stay with him for the rest of his life. Then he turned and walked out.

Behind him, Fleming’s voice, measured and cold: “You will not see him again. We leave for Mars soon. Forget this distraction.”

Mikael didn’t cry until he was home, alone, in his room, where no one could hear it.

CHAPTER V The Disorder

Depression has a particular texture when it arrives after a specific event rather than slowly, as a climate. It is more recognizable and harder to argue with. Mikael stopped attending lectures. He stopped answering questions at the expected intervals. He existed in a suspended state in which the apartment felt smaller than it was and time, already difficult for him, became structureless entirely.

Frieda eventually sat across from him at the kitchen table one morning and told him she had made an appointment. The doctor’s name was Dr. Castellanos. A psychiatrist who still practiced, in an office two floors below a building whose facade was crumbling but whose interior was maintained.

In the third session, while discussing his depression, Dr. Castellanos asked a question that had nothing to do with Georgia.

“When did you last see her?”

“Two days ago.”

“What day was that?”

Mikael opened his mouth. Then stopped. The event was perfectly clear—what she was wearing, what she said, the texture of the chair—but the day of the week had no purchase in his mind. He searched and found nothing. Not fog. Just absence, clean and absolute.

“I…” He frowned. “Wednesday? Or it might have been—”

“What day is today, Mikael?”

He looked at the calendar on the wall. The numbers were there but they did not connect to anything. He had been navigating this all his life by other means—by routine, by watching what others did, by asking questions framed as something else. It occurred to him, distantly, that he had been doing this for as long as he could remember and had never stopped to examine why.

“I don’t know,” he said.

The tests that followed were careful and thorough. At the end of them, Dr. Castellanos leaned back and said, with the gentleness of someone delivering a diagnosis they have been uncertain about until this moment:

“You have a condition called Chronological Dissociation Disorder. Your brain cannot anchor memories to specific points in time. You remember events with perfect clarity—what happened, in what sequence relative to itself. But you cannot reliably know when those events occurred. Not the date, not the day of the week. Sometimes not even whether something happened recently or years ago.”

“Can it be treated?”

“We don’t know enough about it. It’s almost certainly congenital—you’ve had this your entire life, Mikael. You’ve simply developed very effective coping mechanisms.”

Mikael sat with this. He thought of all the times Frieda had reminded him when something was, the way he had built his life around fixed points rather than calendars. Not clumsiness. Not poor memory. A particular kind of mind that moved through time differently than most.

He did not know yet that this was the most important thing about him.

Frieda, when he told her, put her hand over his and said: “I worried it was something else. I’m glad it has a name.”

She said this with a particular quietness. He did not ask what else she had worried it might be.

CHAPTER VI The Final Goodbye

Georgia found him before he could find her. A message arrived—no greeting, just a location. The small park near campus. A bench by the wall where the dust collected in the corner and no one ever sat.

She was already there when he arrived. Her eyes were red but she was composed in the way she was always composed when she was the most furious—the anger had organized itself into something quiet and cold and terribly focused.

“They moved up the departure.”

He sat beside her. He didn’t speak.

“They know I’d drag it out if they gave me time. So they didn’t. We leave for the launch facility in two days.” She looked at her hands. “I told them I won’t go. They didn’t believe me. They were right not to—I’ve got nothing to threaten them with. I’m not going to disappear on a dying planet out of spite. They know I’ll get on the ship.”

“Then get on the ship.”

She looked at him sharply.

“Get on the ship,” he said again. “You have research that matters. You know about the 1973 incident. On Mars, you’ll be close to whatever they’re actually doing with what Harrens found. You can keep going.”

“I don’t want to keep going without you.”

He didn’t say anything to that, because there was nothing useful to say and he refused to say something not useful just to fill the quiet.

They heard the car before they saw it. Dr. Fleming Engel and Dr. Helena Matthews crossed the park with the particular purposefulness of people who had decided to prevent something. Georgia’s mother took her arm. Georgia pulled against it once, then stopped—not surrender, Mikael understood, just the decision not to make this more painful than it had to be.

“I love you,” Mikael said.

“I love you too,” she called back. The car door was already open. “I’ll find a way. I promise.”

The car door closed. Through the window, her palm pressed against the glass. He raised his hand. Then the car moved and the distance became too great for the gesture to mean anything, and then it was gone.

At the launch facility, Mikael stood on the observation level with thousands of people who had no tickets. He found her in the crowd below. She looked up at the observation deck once, as if she knew he was there. She pressed her hand against her heart. He pressed his against his chest.

The ships launched one by one. The noise was tremendous and the fire scorched the grey air and the dust swirled. Mikael watched until the last ship became a bright point moving away from everything, and then the sky was just grey again and he was still standing there.

He went home and did not leave his room for weeks.

CHAPTER VII The Radio

Time passed. Mikael could not say how much. Frieda’s gentle persistence eventually pulled him back to lectures, and the routine of attending created a kind of structure that resembled living even when it was not quite that.

He was at his desk studying when the broadcast interrupted.

The radio shifted from whatever it had been playing to a news bulletin in the standard tone of breaking information.

“Colonial authorities on Mars have confirmed the first violent death in the history of the Mars settlement. The victim, twenty-five-year-old Georgia Engel, was employed as a research secretary at the Mars Stem Research Centre. Her body was discovered in a water treatment facility following reports of discoloration in the local water supply. Maintenance crews found evidence of a violent incident. Water from the affected facility had been distributed to approximately fifteen thousand residents before the contamination was identified. Colonial authorities have classified the incident as a workplace accident pending investigation—”

Mikael’s textbook fell from his hands.

Georgia. The Mars Stem Research Centre. Georgia had gone looking for the truth about what the government was hiding, and she had found it, and they had put her body in a water tank and called it an accident.

When awareness returned, he was on the floor, and his mother was holding him, and the radio was still talking about something else entirely, the way the world continued regardless.

He did not know yet about Elimination Order EO-004. He did not know about Commissioner Obi, or the Custodians of Continuity, or his father-in-law’s name on a classified list that had guaranteed his family’s survival while condemning everyone else’s. He would learn all of this later, in a classified archive two floors below where he was standing in that moment, and it would not bring him any peace. Because what Mikael needed was not information. What he needed was impossible. And no file could give him that.

That night, he sat very still in his room for a long time. Then he got up and went to the desk and opened the oldest notebook—the one labeled Age 7–9—and looked at the first sketch he had ever made of the purple-armored figure.

He had always known, in some wordless way, that the dreams were about something real. He had just never understood what.

CHAPTER VIII The Second Loss

Mikael tried to move forward. He attended classes, though barely present. He existed without living. The pinboard on his wall grew more chaotic—new strings added at strange angles, new questions he couldn’t answer, new sketches of Kaelim that had taken on a quality of urgency he didn’t understand.

Then his phone buzzed. The family doctor. Frieda had been admitted to the hospital. An emergency.

He ran. Three buses felt like eternity. When he reached the ward, the doctor’s face told him everything.

“I’m sorry, Mikael.” The words arranged themselves in the air with a terrible neatness. “Your mother had a degenerative eye condition. Untreated for years—she never mentioned symptoms, never came in for follow-ups. By the time the pain became unbearable, it had already progressed too far. The treatment would have cost more than ten thousand dollars. We think she chose not to pursue it.”

Mikael pushed past into the room. Frieda lay still on the bed. When he took her hand, it was already cool.

He sat for hours as light faded outside, as the world turned without her. Frieda, who had sacrificed her family to raise him. Who had let her boyfriend leave rather than give up the child she’d taken in from a hospital corridor. Who had quietly carried an eye disease for years, diverting the treatment money into his education. He had not known. She had not told him. She had been strong for him through everything, and died alone because she didn’t want to be a burden.

He did not know she had been there the night he was born. He did not know she was the nurse who had held a newborn infant and wept at a dying mother’s bedside and made a decision that would shape everything. He would not know this for a long time. And when he found out, it would be in the worst possible way.

That night, he prepared to end everything. He had the means. He had no reason not to. Nothing left.

But then he fell asleep, and in his dreams, something extraordinary happened.

CHAPTER IX The Dream That Changed Everything

In the dream, Georgia and Frieda stood before him in a light that had no source. They were smiling—not with sadness, but with something that had survived sadness and come out the other side.

Georgia spoke first. Her voice carried the same clear deadpan quality it always had, except now it echoed as though it were passing through distances he couldn’t measure. “You can still reach us, Mikael.”

Frieda took his hand with her cool, familiar grip. “Live. Get up. Come back to us.”

Together their voices harmonized: “TRAVEL BACK TO US!”

Mikael woke with a gasp. Travel back. Not “come back” in death. Not “remember” in grief. Travel back. Through time. To when they were still alive. To when he could change what happened.

It was impossible. Insane. Time travel was a theoretical framework, not a technology. The mathematics had never resolved into anything workable. No one had ever built such a device.

But what did Mikael have left to lose?

The next morning, Mikael withdrew from Environmental Studies and applied to the physics department, quantum mechanics track.

When the advisor asked why, he looked them in the eye and said: “Because I’m going to make time travel possible.”

The advisor laughed. Mikael didn’t.

Somewhere on the pinboard behind him, the sketch of Kaelim watched from the wall.

CHAPTER X The Obsession

For the next fifteen years, Mikael existed in pure, focused obsession. He had no natural talent for quantum physics, but what he lacked in ability he made up for with desperation. Eighteen hours a day. Skipped meals. Forgotten sleep.

The secret turned out to be his disorder. What had been a handicap became a strange asset. While other physicists struggled to conceptualize time as anything but linear progression, Mikael’s brain naturally perceived it as fluid. Past, present, and future didn’t exist on a neat timeline in his experience—they were interconnected in ways he could sense intuitively, the way a blind person develops extraordinary sensitivity in the remaining senses. He couldn’t anchor himself to when, so he had spent a lifetime understanding time as a medium rather than a sequence. That intuition became his primary research tool.

By the third year he had surpassed his peers. By the sixth he was publishing papers that made senior physicists reconsider long-held assumptions. By the twelfth, his PhD dissertation on quantum temporal displacement left the committee in a silence that lasted several minutes before anyone spoke.

At thirty-seven, Dr. Mikael Carver—he had never taken his mother’s name formally, had always simply been Mikael, and would only discover later the significance of that surname—was appointed to the United Earth Space Agency’s research division and assigned to Mars.

The dreams of Kaelim intensified on Mars. Clearer now. More specific. The purple-armored figure standing at the edge of his empire, turning to look directly at Mikael across some distance that was not spatial but temporal. Each dream a fragment. Each fragment a piece of something vast that he could not yet assemble into a shape he recognized.

He had brought all the notebooks. He started new ones.

He had a time machine to build. He was not certain yet what he was building it toward. The dreams were beginning to suggest an answer he was not ready to hear.

CHAPTER XI The Mockery, and the Machine

On his first day presenting to the assembled UESA research staff on Mars, Mikael explained that he intended to build a functioning time machine.

The room erupted in laughter.

“The Time Maniac!” someone called. The name stuck. He acquired others: The T Guy, Doctor Impossible, the man who was going to bring back yesterday’s coffee. Casual, constant ridicule, the kind that doesn’t require malice—just the certainty that something is obviously wrong.

Mikael endured in silence. He requested a private laboratory away from the main research areas. The director granted the request, probably relieved to keep the eccentric contained.

Perfect. That was exactly what Mikael wanted.

For the next twenty years, he disappeared into his lab. He emerged only for mandatory meetings. His colleagues assumed he was wasting time and resources. They had no idea he was succeeding.

The device he built was nothing like science fiction had imagined. It was not a booth, not a vortex, not a massive spinning apparatus. It looked, at rest, like an antique pocket watch in brass and copper, the kind of thing a collector might keep in a drawer. Activate it with the side button and the watch face split open, revealing a holographic interface over mechanical gears that moved with visible precision—steampunk aesthetics merged with physics that should have been impossible. A rotating bezel controlled time increments in hours and minutes. Pull the crown out and it shifted to days, months, years. Confirm the destination by pressing crown and button simultaneously simultaneously. The watch glowed faintly purple when charged—a property Mikael did not yet understand and would only understand much later.

It connected via quantum entanglement to a briefcase-sized charging unit. One use per charge. Twenty-four hours to recharge. Transferred consciousness only, not physical mass—the body rebuilt itself at the destination from local matter, restored to the age and condition of the traveler at that point in time.

When the first test particle displaced backward thirty seconds and coexisted with its past self, Mikael sat down on the laboratory floor and wept.

Twenty years. And then the mockers fell silent.

“I didn’t build this for recognition,” he told the assembled scientific community at the award ceremony that followed. “I built it for love. And now I’m going to use it.”

He returned to his laboratory, locked the door, and prepared to travel back in time. He had no idea what was waiting for him.

CHAPTER XII The Archive

Before he used the machine, Mikael did one other thing.

He had been tracking Georgia’s research for fifteen years—the files she had gathered, the documents her mother had unknowingly brought home, the incomplete picture her murder had interrupted. He had followed the thread from the university study room to the classified archive levels of the UESA research facility on Mars, mapping access patterns quietly, waiting.

A researcher named Dr. Emeka Solaris stopped him in a service corridor one afternoon. A heavy-framed man, warm in person, with the permanently distracted quality of someone whose mind was always elsewhere. He said: “I know what you found. I know what you’re looking for. I can tell you that you’re right about all of it. The woman who died in the water treatment facility—I tried to stop it. I failed.”

He pressed a classified access code into Mikael’s hand. He did not explain why. He walked away.

Mikael used the code. He reached Level 4. He found everything.

The complete Harrens record—not Georgia’s fragments, but the original. The deep-earth facility. March 14, 1973. A geophysicist who tried to stop them and fell into the thing he was trying to protect. The Stem, fractured at its centre. Earth’s life support system, cracked like a spine severed. The seismic events across three continents within six hours. The eleven surviving witnesses, dispersed within the week. The incident report, three sentences long, mentioning no names.

He found the Continuity Framework. The Custodians of Continuity. The GMMM price criteria, designed in a private negotiation between the UEC and three industrial conglomerates to ensure Mars would be founded as a wealthy colony.

He found Elimination Order EO-004. Commissioner Obi’s name. The forty-eight-hour window. The forged maintenance request. Workplace accident.

He found the Continuity Roster. He found Dr. Fleming Engel’s name on it. He understood then that the dinner had not been cruelty as a personality failing. It had been cruelty as participation in a system that had already decided which lives were acceptable collateral. Fleming had known his daughter was being watched. Moving her to Mars was damage control. He had not expected her to get inside the facility. He had not ordered her death. He had simply been so thoroughly inside the machine that he could not tell the difference between those two things.

He found Georgia’s UEC Academic Surveillance File. Opened in 2041—when she was sixteen. Before she ever met him. She had been watched from the moment she began researching the 1973 incident. The surveillance file that had been following her since she was a teenager was the file that had flagged her reaction in the classified briefing room as a critical disclosure threat and triggered the elimination order.

She had been dead from the moment she first opened her mother’s archive.

He found the Stem fragment storage records. Two floors below where he was standing. He could take it. He did not. He had other plans.

He copied everything. He locked the drive. He returned to his lab.

He did nothing with the files. He had in his hands the proof that would tear the Custodians apart, vindicate Georgia, potentially save millions of lives. He did nothing, because Georgia was already dead, and Frieda was already dead, and the truth was not what he needed. What he needed was impossible. And the machine was the only thing in the world that pretended it might not be.

CHAPTER XIII The Stem

The classified briefing the UESA director gave him changed everything he thought he understood about why he was here.

Every planet, the director explained, had a Stem. Not the same kind—each was shaped by its planet’s composition, its history, its particular chemistry. Earth’s Stem was green, wound through the deep mantle like a root system older than anything alive on the surface. Mars’s was brownish-orange, slower, more patient. Neptune’s was dark blue ice running through the frozen interior. Each one the life support system of its world, the thing that made the environment work, the organism at the base of everything.

Earth’s Stem had been broken on March 14, 1973, when Dr. Daryl Harrens fell into it during a physical struggle over an extraction order, absorbed an enormous quantity of Stem energy in the seconds before his body gave out, and the catastrophic drain fractured the organism at its centre. The energy burst that followed caused seismic events across three continents. The Stem—cracked, severed through the middle like a spine cut—could no longer maintain the planet’s environmental balance. The collapse that followed had been inevitable from that moment forward.

The UESA had been studying the Mars Stem for fifteen years. They had also quietly recovered the small fragment partially freed during the original 1973 drilling, and transported it to a classified storage facility.

Two floors below where Mikael was now standing.

He had known it was there. He had chosen not to take it earlier. But now he understood something he hadn’t understood before: the Stem attracted certain individuals. People with specific neurological patterns. People whose brains perceived reality differently.

People with Chronological Dissociation Disorder.

The pull had been building since he arrived on Mars. He had taken it for focus, for obsession, for grief without an outlet. Now he understood it for what it was.

One night, working late and alone in the sub-level corridor, he stopped outside the storage chamber. The pull was stronger than rational thought. He had read what happened to Harrens. He understood the mechanism. He understood the risk.

He placed his hand against the containment surface anyway.

The Stem fragment, behind its shielding, responded. Not the catastrophic flood of energy that had destroyed Harrens—Mikael’s disorder, his particular way of perceiving time as fluid rather than linear, gave him something Harrens hadn’t had. He could absorb without shattering. The energy moved through him and settled.

When he pulled his hand back, purple light crackled briefly across his skin and then vanished. He stood very still for a long moment.

He had been marked. Changed. And in the moment of contact, the Stem had shown him something—a vision, a path, a terrible shape of what was going to happen.

He understood now why the watch glowed purple when charged. He understood now what the color meant, and what it didn’t mean. The color a person took from Stem energy was random—it had nothing to do with the color of the Stem itself. His was purple. The Stem had not given him that color. It had simply revealed it.

And in the revelation, he understood, finally, what the dreams had been trying to tell him for eighteen years.

CHAPTER XIV The Terrible Mistake

The dreams weren’t dreams. They were memories.

Memory leaks from other versions of himself across other timelines—dozens of them, hundreds of them, almost all of whom had made the same choice he was about to make and followed the same path forward. Mikael 1 had been the first, the one without any dreams, the one who had no warning. Every Mikael after him had dreamed of what Mikael 1 became. The dreams were non-linear because time travel disrupts chronological order—he had been dreaming of Kaelim’s middle life, his death, his birth in the wrong sequence, because that was the order in which the information had leaked backward through the fractures in causality.

Kaelim was him. Kaelim had always been him. The purple armor, the empire in space, the woman named Gaia with alligator eyes, the sadness old enough to have become something else—all of it was his future, already lived, already dreamed, already waiting.

And the terrible mistake was not a mistake.

Mikael set the temporal coordinates with trembling hands. He wanted to go back to when he first met Georgia, to love her properly from the beginning, to prevent the sequence of events that led to her murder. But his Chronological Dissociation Disorder made precise dating impossible. He knew approximately when. He could not know exactly. He input his best estimate.

The machine activated. Reality twisted. Consciousness streamed backward through time.

When the world solidified, he was young again—twenty-five, his body restored to what it had been before the decades of obsession wore it down. He stood on a familiar street, but something was wrong. The buildings looked older. The models of the cars. The advertisements for products he had never heard of.

He had miscalculated. He had gone back further than he intended, past when he had met Georgia, past when Georgia had been born. He was in the wrong generation entirely.

Then he saw her. A young woman with Georgia’s face, walking with books clutched to her chest. Dark hair, brown eyes. The same particular way of carrying herself. The same watchful intelligence.

He approached. They talked. She studied environmental science. She shared his passion for saving the world. She was brilliant and funny and kind. She had hyperflexible joints, the same as him. She had parents who had adopted her and didn’t know she knew. She had wealthy adoptive parents who disapproved of her activism.

Her name, she told him, was Georgia.

Overwhelmed with joy at what felt like a miracle, Mikael did not question it. He had been warned of the mistake in his dreams, across eighteen years of fragments, and his disorder had simply overridden the warning the way it always overrode everything temporal. He couldn’t hold the date. He couldn’t hold the distance.

He courted her. They fell in love. It felt like coming home.

They married young. Georgia became pregnant with twins. Mikael was deliriously happy, convinced he had succeeded.

The day the twins were born, he entered the hospital room with flowers. The doctor congratulated him on his son and daughter. He crossed the threshold and stopped.

In the corner, a young nurse cradled two newborns. Her name tag read: Frieda Carver.

Georgia, pale against the pillows, smiled at him with a love so complete it shattered him. “I named them Mikael and Georgia. Like us. So they’ll always have each other.”

Mikael saw the calendar on the wall. The date. The year. His disorder had betrayed him catastrophically. He hadn’t gone back to when he met his girlfriend Georgia. He had gone back an entire generation. He had met, courted, and married his own biological mother.

The baby boy that nurse Frieda was holding was him. The baby girl was the child who would grow up to become his girlfriend, his partner, the woman he loved—his twin sister.

He was his own father. The paradox was complete and horrifying and sealed.

Georgia died in childbirth minutes later. Frieda, who had watched the entire sequence unfold—who had seen Mikael’s biological mother die, had seen his biological father run from the room in horror, had heard Georgia name the children with her last breath—took baby Mikael home. She could not afford both. She was twenty-seven years old, alone, newly rejected by her family for taking in an orphan. She took one. Baby Georgia was sent to a state facility, where she was eventually adopted by a wealthy family named Engel—a neural surgeon named Fleming and an archaeologist named Helena.

The loop closed. The loop had always been closed. It was closing right now and it had already been closed for thirty-two years in the future Mikael had come from.

Mikael ran from the hospital. He ran until he couldn’t run anymore, and then he collapsed in an alley in a time that was not his, and screamed at the grey sky until he had nothing left.

CHAPTER XV Kaelim

He did not go back. The charging unit required twenty-four hours to recharge. By the time it was ready, Mikael had made a decision.

He could not undo what had happened. He could not unfather himself. He could not unmurder his own mother with the weight of his love for someone who had been his sister all along. He could not unlearn that the woman he had loved as a girlfriend and the woman he had loved as a mother were the same person, and that his love for them both was genuine and innocent and irrelevant to the horror it had created.

The Stem energy inside him had changed what was possible. He was stronger than he had been. He would live longer than he should. And he had seen, in the moment the Stem showed him the path forward, what the shape of it was.

He fled to a spaceport. He did not go gently. He gathered people—refugees, outcasts, the kind of people who had been told by the GMMM and the Continuity Roster and the crumbling buildings of a dying Earth that their lives were acceptable losses. He offered them something no one else was offering: honesty about what had happened, and a destination that wasn’t Mars.

He stole ships. He built more. He established territory in the spaces between planetary systems where the UEC had no reach. The purple energy was controlled now—not a glow but a tool, extending from his hands as whips of force, granting him invisibility when he needed it, keeping him alive far beyond the normal human span.

Decades passed. The purple-armored figure from eighteen years of dreams became real. Became legend. Became Kaelim—the name the empire had given him, the name that spread through colonized space as a symbol of something the UEC could not categorize as entirely evil or entirely good.

He recruited a woman named Gaia on a destroyed planet called Xylaris. She was shorter than a human, dark greenish-blue, with the alligator eyes from his oldest dreams, circular dark spots across her skin, brown hair. She read minds by closing her eyes and entering the consciousness of anyone within fifteen meters as an invisible presence in their thoughts. She approached him the way she approached everyone she encountered—for survival and curiosity.

She read his mind once. Then she stayed.

“You have the entire timeline on your back,” she said, the first time she described what she had seen. “Every version of you that ever did this. You’re not just one man. You’re a convergence point.”

“I know,” Kaelim said.

“You’ve been waiting for something. What is it?”

He looked out at the stars. “My younger self. He’s coming. He’s full of rage at what I’ve done, and he doesn’t understand yet that what I’ve done was the only way forward. And when he arrives, everything will finally be able to end.”

CHAPTER XVI The Second Self

Of the thousand timelines that had branched from Mikael 1’s first journey, almost all followed the same arc. They made the mistake. They became Kaelim. They waited. The end was always the same.

Almost all.

There was one version of Mikael who had dreamed more clearly than the others. Who had understood the shape of the trap before stepping into it. Who had looked at the dreams of Kaelim—the empire, the isolation, the horror of what he had become and what he had unknowingly created—and made a different calculation.

This Mikael was more analytical and less emotional in his grief. Not harder—his grief was just as real—but it had organized itself into something colder. He had lost Georgia and Frieda. He had received the dreams. He had understood that using the machine would lead to the paradox. And he had decided that the right response to this was not to become the monster but to kill the monster before it had a chance to propagate another iteration of the loop.

He did not time travel. He calculated where Kaelim’s empire would be, and he went there the long way, aging in real time, driven by a cold determination that had the texture of grief and the architecture of rage.

He arrived in Kaelim’s throne room in the sixty-third year of Kaelim’s empire. He was older. He was furious. He understood, in a way the time-traveling versions of himself had never understood, that the dreams were not a path to salvation but a trap. That every Mikael who used the machine was adding another iteration to a loop that Time itself was exhausted by.

He had come to end it.

Kaelim let him through. Ordered his soldiers to stand down. Watched his other self cross the throne room floor with pure rage in his eyes and a cold determination underneath it, and felt something he hadn’t felt in decades: recognition. Kinship. The strange warmth of being truly seen by the only person in existence who could see him.

“You know what you did,” the other Mikael said. “You married her. You fathered yourself. You made yourself your own father and your girlfriend your mother and you destroyed everything you loved to build this empire of nothing.”

“Yes,” Kaelim said.

“And you want me to kill you. You’ve been waiting for someone to kill you so the loop can reset.”

“Yes.”

The other Mikael’s jaw tightened. “Then I won’t give you a peaceful death. I’ll give you what you gave the timeline: a reckoning.”

They fought. Kaelim defended but did not truly attack—he knew how this ended, he had been dreaming toward this moment for longer than his other self had been alive. The other Mikael’s pure rage drove him forward past every defense. Gaia tried to intervene. She was caught in the crossfire of something she loved and couldn’t stop, and she fell.

The killing blow landed.

As Kaelim fell, he smiled. “Good,” he whispered. “Now it’s complete.”

The other Mikael dropped to his knees beside the body of himself. Gaia was dying nearby. The empire’s soldiers stood frozen, uncertain what they had just witnessed. And then reality fractured at every seam—the paradox fully realized, the causality loop complete and impossible, the universe’s rules broken past the point of natural repair.

And then Time itself arrived.

CHAPTER XVII The God of Time

It manifested as a voice. Not a form—no body, no face, no gender. Just presence filling the throne room, and then a voice that was one clear male voice echoing as though it were bouncing off every moment that had ever existed.

Everything stopped. Kaelim’s soldiers froze mid-breath. Gaia’s fading consciousness went still. The other Mikael, kneeling on the floor beside the body of himself, could not move.

THE PARADOX CANNOT SUSTAIN. I OFFER A CHOICE.

The other Mikael managed to speak. “What choice?”

THIS TIMELINE CONTINUES IN ITS BROKEN STATE. THE LOOP ITERATES AGAIN. ANOTHER MIKAEL TRAVELS. ANOTHER KAELIM BUILDS. ANOTHER SECOND SELF COMES. THIS HAS HAPPENED ONE THOUSAND AND SEVEN TIMES.

He sat with that number for a long moment.

OR. THE RESET.

“What happens in the reset?”

A NEW TIMELINE. THE PARADOX NEVER OCCURS. THE STEM IS NOT BROKEN IN 1973. EARTH DOES NOT DIE. MIKAEL CARVER IS BORN TO HIS REAL PARENTS—ANTONIO AND GEORGIA—AND LIVES A LIFE WITHOUT THIS HORROR. THE MEMORIES ARE BURIED. NOT GONE.

“Buried. Not gone.”

THE DREAMS WILL COME, IN THE NEW TIMELINE. QUIETER THAN BEFORE. NOT UNDERSTOOD. HE WILL NOT KNOW WHAT THEY MEAN. BUT THEY WILL BE THERE.

“And Georgia? Frieda?”

THEY WILL LIVE FULL LIVES. GEORGIA WILL BE HIS SISTER, NOT HIS LOVER. FRIEDA WILL BE HIS MOTHER, NOT HIS GUARDIAN. HE WILL HAVE A FAMILY. A GOOD ONE.

The other Mikael looked at the body of Kaelim on the floor. At everything that body had carried for decades. At the price of the loop. At the thousand and six iterations before this one, each one a man who had loved two people and lost them and broken reality trying to reach them again.

“Do it,” he said. “Reset everything. Give him the life he should have had.”

YOUR SACRIFICE BIRTHS A BETTER WORLD.

Reality inverted. The universe collapsed inward to a single point of impossible brightness.

The last coherent thought either Mikael had before everything went white was the same thought, arriving from two directions at once:

Let them be happy. Let them all be happy.

CHAPTER XVIII What the Reset Leaves Behind

The conspiracy was not defeated by the reset. This is the part that the reset did not fix.

In the new timeline, the Unified Earth Council still existed. The Custodians of Continuity still existed. The GMMM still existed, with the same price criteria, the same class-selection architecture, the same Continuity Roster guaranteeing the right people a seat while billions of others were left behind with enough procedural legitimacy surrounding their exclusion that it couldn’t legally be called murder. It could only be called policy.

Commissioner Obi still existed. Minister Tanaka still existed. Director Voss still existed, his silver hair designed for authority, his face doing something he didn’t intend at carefully selected moments.

Harlan Voss still stood in front of cameras and announced humanity’s future with complete sincerity about Mars and complete silence about why Earth had no future.

The Harrens file was still buried. Thomas Carver’s 1891 report from a colonial expedition in Africa, describing a deep-core sample that behaved as though resistant to extraction and caused vivid shared perceptual experiences in the team, was still misfiled in a museum archive somewhere, undiscovered. The private correspondence between three European industrialists in 1924, referencing “the geobiotic structure,” was still in a Swiss archive that no one was looking at.

The truth was still available. Still buried. Still no one’s problem specifically, because the one person with Mikael’s specific combination of knowledge, access, grief-fuelled obsession, and chronological disorder that made time itself feel fluid—the one person who would have eventually reached Level 4 and found everything—was now living a different life. A good one. A quiet one.

This is the reset’s final irony. It saved one man. It left everything else exactly as it was.

Somewhere in the quantum foam between realities, a classified drive containing a complete copy of every Custodian document, including Elimination Order EO-004, sat in UESA quantum storage on Mars. Dr. Emeka Solaris, who had been promoted in the years since, flagged it as corrupted data during a routine audit. He did not delete it. He ate dinner alone that night and thought about a researcher he had given an access code to once, in a service corridor, who had found everything and done nothing, and he wondered what had happened to him.

The drive stayed where it was.

Some discoveries are patient. Some truths wait longer than the people who buried them.

EPILOGUE The New Dawn

Mikael woke in his apartment to sunlight.

Not the grey, dust-filtered approximation of sunlight that fell through the windows of 2050 Earth—actual sunlight, warm and direct, crossing the floor in a rectangle that moved slowly while he lay still and watched it. He sat up slowly, disoriented in the way of someone who has been dreaming something vast and can no longer remember what it was.

He looked at the calendar on the wall. The date was clear and comprehensible, the way dates were for most people—he could read it and know when he was, anchor to it, feel it as a real coordinate rather than a decorative symbol. His disorder was gone. He had not had it since birth in this timeline. He had never needed the pinboard, never needed the notebooks, never needed to build elaborate systems to compensate for the absence of a thing most people took for granted.

Fragments of something drifted through him. Purple light. A woman dying. A figure in armor who kept turning to look at him across an impossible distance. He shook his head slowly. Strange dreams. Old ones. He had been having them since he was young, in a quiet and diminished form, like echoes of something that had never quite fully arrived.

His phone rang. Georgia. His sister Georgia—an environmental scientist, brilliant in her field, still slightly terrible at the violin, still the kind of person who made strong opinions about other people’s coffee orders. “Are you coming to Mum and Dad’s for dinner tonight?”

“Of course,” he said. “I wouldn’t miss it.”

After the call, he stood in his apartment and looked at the photographs on the wall. His parents—Antonio and Georgia senior, warm and real and alive, who had raised him and his sister in a house where no one went hungry, where no one kept secrets from the doctors about painful eye conditions to save money for someone else’s education. His mother’s eyes were fine. His father’s knees were generationally hyperflexible, as were Mikael’s and Georgia’s, and no one made anything of it.

He moved through his morning with the unearned ease of a man whose life had been given back to him without his knowledge of the gift. He made coffee. He sat with the slight melancholy that had followed him since childhood like a shadow without a source, the feeling of something he had forgotten that mattered enormously, the shape of a grief he couldn’t locate.

He opened the drawer of his desk. Inside was a notebook—not leather-bound and worn and labeled by age, just an ordinary notebook he had bought recently. He had been writing in it for the past month. Not dreams exactly. Impressions. A man in purple armor who kept turning to look at him. A dying world with grey skies and constant dust. A woman with brown eyes and a quick crooked smile who ordered his coffee for him and was very sure about everything.

He read the latest entry:

“The dreams persist. I see myself as someone else—someone who carried something I can’t name, something so heavy it had its own gravity. I see cities crumbling. I see a choice being made by a voice I can’t locate, in a language that isn’t words. I wake up and the day is good and the sky is blue and everything is fine and I can’t shake the feeling that it wasn’t always. That something was spent to make it so. That the cost was paid by a version of me I will never be able to thank.”

He closed the notebook. He looked out the window at the green Earth, the blue sky, the world still going.

He did not know what had been done to give him this. He did not know about Kaelim, or the one thousand and seven iterations, or the Second Self, or the voice that had spoken at the end of a paradox older than any institution on Earth. He did not know about Gaia, or the Stem, or the classified drive in UESA quantum storage containing a truth that was still waiting for someone to find it.

He knew only this: the melancholy, the dreams, the shadow of something he couldn’t name. And beneath that, constant and unreasonable and completely structureless—

Gratitude.

For what, he could not say. To whom, he had no idea. But it was there when he woke up and there when he slept, the feeling of a debt he didn’t remember incurring, paid forward into a life he hadn’t earned and couldn’t explain and wouldn’t give back for anything.

Somewhere in the quantum foam between realities, in a space that was not a place, in a moment that was not a time, something that had once been purple and armored and immeasurably tired looked at this life it had purchased with everything it had—and was satisfied.

The story of Mikael Carver came to an end.

Or perhaps, to a beginning.

“Some stories are written in time.
Some are written across time.
This one was written in the space between.”

THE END
